From music file to headphone signal.

The processor reads files and runs the menus. A digital-to-analog converter, or DAC, turns decoded samples into the electrical signal used by the headphone output.

Processor platformRockchip RKnano with two ARM Cortex-M3 cores: one runs the player; the other runs a decoder loaded when needed.
Audio chip ACS43198 · bus address 0x30 (7-bit)
Audio chip BCS43198 · bus address 0x33 (7-bit)
Chip identification · 4 GB unitID 43 19 80 · revision A1 · subrevision 06
Audio transportI2S carries audio samples to the DACs. DMA transfers samples from memory without making the main loop copy each one.
EQSoftware EQ runs on the main processor. The CS43198’s own digital filters and gain controls are separate.

Follow the two-core audio path below. Read the Cirrus Logic CS43198 documentation for chip details, or FiiO’s specifications for player ratings.

How the chip identification was read

Read-only registers 0x010000, 0x010001 and 0x010002 supplied the ID. Registers 0x010004 and 0x010005 supplied revision and subrevision. The driver’s wire-format addresses 0x60 and 0x66 correspond to 7-bit addresses 0x30 and 0x33.

Both chips on a 4 GB ECHO returned 431980 A1.06. A1.06 identifies their hardware revision.

What Gnarlyr unlocks.

The ECHO keeps its processor, DACs, display and battery. Gnarlyr changes how the firmware uses them, adding controls and refining the paths between music, headphones and USB.

  • Guarded changes to the analog output. Stock changes gain while the headphone outputs are powered. Gnarlyr mutes and power-sequences gain changes, mutes around filter changes and guards jack transitions while they settle. Low gain uses the CS43198’s documented setting in place of stock’s reserved value.
  • Separate listening levels. Wired and Bluetooth playback remember their own volume. Four headphone profiles add digital trim, balance and ceilings for wired listening, including USB audio. The two headphone jacks share the DACs; choosing an output selects its volume scale.
  • USB choices on one cable. Move between storage, Charge & play and USB audio. Run from USB power lets the cable supply the player with charging off; a low or unknown battery reading enables charging first. Stock already exposes the supply and charging controls in its DAC mode; Gnarlyr brings them into the player’s USB chooser.
  • A clock you can set. The clock chip keeps time while the player is off. Set the date, local time and UTC offset, choose a 12- or 24-hour display, and open the monthly calendar from Home → Clock.
  • Finer backlight control. Ten brightness levels span 20–100% drive, with a ramp when the backlight turns on. Stock’s six settings top out at 95% drive. The display driver sends changed areas and adjusts its transfer timing when the processor clock changes.
  • Display transfers by DMA. Larger changed regions go straight from memory to the display controller while the main core sleeps. The panel sets the transfer pace; DMA frees processor time for the player. Small regions and transfers that time out use the CPU path.
  • Less activity between tasks. Stock’s main loop busy-polls. Gnarlyr lets idle cores sleep, batches screen-off storage reads and adjusts the main core’s clock to the playback workload. It stops the display controller’s clock while the panel sleeps and turns off an unconnected Bluetooth radio after 30 seconds. Optional standby keeps a paused session ready on the 24 MHz crystal, with a later full shutdown.

These changes describe firmware behavior. Battery-life gains, output levels and audible improvements need measurements and listening checks on the player. FiiO’s power and battery ratings remain the reference above.

How the small hardware does more.

Playing music is a relay: read a file, turn it into samples, keep those samples moving, then turn them into sound. The ECHO splits that work between two processors and dedicated transfer hardware.

  1. 01

    Read and decode.

    Core 0 runs the menus, storage and audio processing. Core 1 runs the loaded compressed-audio decoder; a dedicated hardware accelerator helps reconstruct FLAC audio.

  2. 02

    Keep a buffer ready.

    A ring in RAM holds decoded samples. DMA moves them to I2S at the audio clock’s pace while the processors prepare more.

  3. 03

    Make the signal.

    I2S feeds both CS43198 DACs. They convert samples into the analog signal used by the headphone outputs.

Keeping playback moving

  • Continuous audio transfers. Both stock and Gnarlyr use DMA to move samples without copying each one in the main loop. Gnarlyr clears stale audio on restart, refills before restarting DMA and fades around interrupted playback. Compatible gapless transitions keep the continuous sample stream.
  • Targeted decoder work. Stock decoder modules reference FLAC prediction, MP3 synthesis and transform accelerators. Gnarlyr uses the FLAC prediction block, while its other decoder work uses software and targeted ARM arithmetic.
  • Room for more features. RAM is reused for code modules: scanning, extra screens and USB need not all live in memory at once. Scanning can use decoder RAM while playback is stopped. The display stores its own picture, avoiding a full-screen framebuffer in main RAM.

Screen, backlight and buttons.

The display stores its own picture. The processor sends changed areas, helping the interface fit within the ECHO’s memory budget.

  • Display protocol. The driver uses an ST7796S-style command set and 16-bit RGB565 pixel transfers. Its startup sequence matches the stock firmware’s 24 display-initialization commands and their data.
  • Landscape and portrait. Stock’s landscape window has a 49-row offset. Portrait changes the orientation bits and applies that offset to the other axis.
  • Backlight. A 25 kHz PWM signal on GPIO2.4 controls brightness by rapidly switching the backlight on and off.
  • Buttons. Volume, Play, Menu and Back share a resistor ladder. An ADC, a voltage-measuring input, reads the different voltages so the player can identify each button. Power has its own input.

USB, power and Bluetooth.

USB storage exposes the music drives to your computer. The USB mode chooser lets you return to listening or reconnect storage on the same cable. The firmware also provides a USB audio path; a separate module handles Bluetooth headphones.

  • USB controller. A DesignWare USB 2.0 OTG controller. Storage can negotiate high speed and retry at full speed if the first connection does not become usable.
  • Mode selection. Open USB mode from Home or Settings. Eject both drives before leaving storage, then choose Charge & play without unplugging. The current track, position and queue are kept. Run from USB power uses the cable without charging; low or unknown battery readings turn charging back on first.
  • USB packets and audio DMA. The USB driver moves packets through the controller’s hardware queues. In DAC mode, the audio output still uses its DMA-fed ring; the computer has already decoded the music.
  • A different USB stack. Gnarlyr’s storage and audio code share a loaded module region. Storage activity, ejects and pending writes are tracked before handing the drives back to the player. The cable can keep supplying power throughout.
  • Separate drives. Internal storage and an inserted microSD card appear as separate drives. Eject every drive present at connection to return storage to the player.
  • Audio mode. The high-speed USB Audio Class 2.0 path offers 16/24/32-bit PCM at 44.1–192 kHz. Full-speed Class 1.0 fallback offers 16/24-bit at 44.1/48 kHz. DAC volume is remembered separately. Formats and host validation →
  • Cable detection. GPIO2.0 controls taking power from USB and GPIO0.14 controls charging. Gnarlyr keeps these power-control signals in sync with the cable state.
  • Bluetooth module. A separate module handles wireless audio. The processor controls it with short commands over UART1 at 115,200 baud. FiiO specifies SBC transmission to headphones.

Storage and working memory.

Storage keeps music and firmware when the player is off. RAM holds working data while it runs. Firmware has two separate storage slots; trial updates keep the previous version in slot 2 while the candidate runs from slot 1. Confirming the new version copies it into the backup slot. First installations and stock recovery still write and verify both slots.

Internal storageeMMC with two firmware slots, a SAVE area and the user’s FAT32 volume. The microSD card is separate.
Main working RAMCore 0 data window: 0x03004000–0x0304C000 · 288 KiB of mapped space. Boot loading and reserved code banks constrain what the app can use.
Decoder RAMCore 1 region: 0x01000000–0x0105FFFF · 384 KiB.
Shared high RAM0x0307A000–0x0309EFFF · 148 KiB, used for overlays and temporary work.
Main code location0x03050000. Extra screens and USB work can load into a bounded module region.
Battery readingADC channel 0 measures the rail powering the player. On battery this reflects the cell; USB power can lift the rail above the cell voltage.

Gnarlyr therefore distinguishes Charging, Charged and USB power. It avoids presenting a USB-powered rail reading as the battery’s voltage.

These working regions come from the firmware map and stock-code analysis; they do not specify the ECHO’s total RAM capacity.

Beyond the ECHO

Other players. New possibilities.

A port brings Gnarlyr to a different player. Similar chips give us a starting point, but each player needs its own screen, button, audio and power support.

Gnarlyr currently targets the full-size Snowsky ECHO. These are candidates for future investigation; use ECHO firmware images only on the full-size ECHO.

Close relative

Snowsky ECHO Mini

Why look here?
Related Rockchip firmware and existing community work make the Mini a useful starting point. FiiO lists two CS43131 DACs and a 170 × 320 colour screen.
What needs adapting?
The DAC driver, display layout, controls and board wiring. Storage variants need separate checks of their firmware layout and recovery procedure.

Cross-brand candidate

Hidizs AP30 Music Boy

Why look here?
Hidizs specifies an RKNANOD processor and CS43198 DAC, sharing the ECHO’s processor family and DAC model. It also has microSD, USB DAC mode and 3.5 mm / 4.4 mm outputs.
What needs adapting?
The 296 × 240 screen, directional buttons, volume wheel and RT6863 amplifier. Its boot process, memory map and board wiring need investigation before bringing Gnarlyr up.

Smaller-screen candidate

Snowsky ECHO Nano

Why look here?
FiiO lists a Rockchip RK NanoD processor and CS43131 DAC. Community firmware patches already document parts of its software and image format.
What needs adapting?
The 0.91-inch OLED and scroll wheel call for a simpler interface. Audio, storage, power and update handling would also need a Nano-specific implementation.

Older players worth investigating

The xDuoo Nano D3 and IQQ C18 are additional leads from the hardware shortlist. The next step is to verify their exact processor, memory, display and audio circuitry from board or firmware evidence, then assess how much Gnarlyr could reuse. xDuoo’s firmware archive ↗ provides a starting point for the D3.

What turns a candidate into a supported player?

First establish a reliable recovery route and a model-specific firmware image. Then bring up the screen, buttons, storage and audio, and check playback, USB transfers, charging and saved settings on the actual player. Shared chips help us choose where to look; those checks establish support.
